Finding the part is not the hard problem.

Qualifying the second company that can make it, before the first one fails, is.

A safety-critical component lets go on a fly-in site. The nearest replacement is not on this continent, and neither is the only supplier who has ever made it.

Emergency work costs more than the same work planned, and freight into a remote site costs more and takes longer than freight anywhere else. Availability, cost per tonne and unplanned downtime hours absorb all of it.

Critical spares and min-max cover the part you predicted. Neither covers a single qualified source going quiet on a safety-critical item.

Participation commitments get audited

Obligation

In a modern treaty area, participation obligations are legally enforceable and administered by named bodies. Procurement documents screen out contractors with no credible participation strategy early, well before technical evaluation.

ConvergX does not certify Indigenous business status, does not administer a participation plan, and does not audit anyone's committed spend.

What it does is the part that stalls: finding a certified Indigenous business or a local contractor that can actually hold the scope, and vetting them before the commitment is reported against.

Both are the same problem

Qualification

A second source on a safety-critical item and a participation commitment you can demonstrate both come down to qualifying a company nobody on site has worked with.

ConvergX does that by hand, one requirement at a time. A named organisation reviews the company on the other side and decides whether the introduction happens.

The site list is built from whoever supplied the last shutdown. A company that has never been on it has nothing in your systems to be read.
